\begin{abstract}
\bfseries
Free riders obtain the shared federated model without performing local
training, while fabricated responses can resemble genuine updates.
BaitFL audits this behavior through undisclosed model challenges,
update norms and layer-level profiles. Geometric tests and repeated fresh
evidence govern removal, while bounded review limits exclusion when
the evidence remains inconclusive. The server requires neither an
auxiliary detector nor client-reported loss or attack identities.
Experiments across three random seeds cover MNIST and CIFAR-10, IID and
non-IID partitions, four non-adaptive fabrication strategies, and 30\%
and 40\% free riders. All evaluated free riders are removed, giving
\MultiRecall\% recall across \ReviewRunCount{} runs. There are \MultiFP{}
honest removals, concentrated in \ReviewAffectedRuns{} runs. In separately
timed 100-round runs, server-side checking accounted for 0.58\% of total
computation time on MNIST and 0.89\% on CIFAR-10.
BaitFL is lightweight in server computation
for the evaluated population of 100 clients. Even IID settings withhold
about 11\% of honest responses through auditing. Withheld contributions
and honest-response overlap remain costs of the method, which requires
visibility of individual client responses.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Federated learning (FL) allows multiple clients to train a shared model
without handing over their local data. In each communication round, the
server distributes the current global model, the clients update it using
their own data, and the server aggregates the returned updates into a
revised model~\cite{mcmahan2017communication}. Since raw data stays with
its owner, participants can collaborate without first creating a central
repository of their records. Keeping data local does not remove every
privacy risk, as shared updates can still reveal information about
training data~\cite{kairouz2021advances,zhu2019leakage,melis2019leakage}.
Exchanging updates instead of
datasets also avoids moving large volumes of local data, although repeated
model exchange remains a significant communication cost in
FL~\cite{mcmahan2017communication}. Effective collaboration depends on clients
performing useful local training, even though the server cannot directly
observe that work.

Like other distributed systems, FL is open to misconduct. Label-flipping and
model-poisoning attacks, for example, alter local data or submitted updates
in order to degrade the global model or introduce targeted
behavior~\cite{kairouz2021advances,bagdasaryan2020backdoor}. Free riding is
a quieter problem. The participant's primary aim is to obtain the shared
model without paying the cost of local
training~\cite{lin2019freeriders,fraboni2021freerider}. A client may lack
useful data or computational resources, or may simply choose to conserve
them. Instead of training, it can return an unchanged model, fabricate a
noisy response, or build an update from models received in earlier rounds.
Such responses may resemble what the server expects, so they need not
visibly disrupt learning and can evade checks aimed at overtly harmful
updates. Free riding shifts the training burden to honest participants. When
a client withholds useful data and computation, the federation also loses a
contribution that could have improved the shared model.
Figure~\ref{fig:free-rider-attack} illustrates this behavior: the free rider
receives the shared model alongside the training clients but returns a
fabricated response instead of performing local optimization.

Detecting this omission is difficult because unusual updates are not limited
to free riders. Honest clients differ in sample sizes, class distributions,
and local learning trajectories, particularly under non-IID
data~\cite{kairouz2021advances}. A small update can therefore reflect
legitimate training, while a fabricated one can look like an ordinary
contribution. The server has to tell these cases apart without observing
local optimization. Existing approaches rely on learned anomaly models,
parameter audits, or evidence obtained through inference
attacks~\cite{lin2019freeriders,wang2024pass,recasens2026frida}. This raises a
practical question: can lightweight server-side checks detect free riding
without training an auxiliary detector? There is also a cost to being wrong.
Withholding an honest client's updates can reduce its contribution to the shared
model, even if it is never permanently removed. A detector must therefore
consider both the evidence needed for removal and the duration of temporary
exclusion.

To address this problem, we propose \emph{BaitFL}, a lightweight trap-based
method for detecting and mitigating free riders in federated learning.
Across \ReviewRunCount{} evaluated runs, it removes all tested free riders,
with \MultiFP{} honest removals in \ReviewAffectedRuns{} runs. Checking
accounts for less than 1\% of computation in the instrumented executions.

\noindent{The main contributions of this work are as follows:}
\begin{itemize} %[leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item \textbf{Lightweight server-side detection.} We develop a challenge-based
detector using update norms and tensor profiles, without training an auxiliary
detection model or accessing clients' private training data.
\item \textbf{Confirmation using repeated evidence.} We design a decision
mechanism that separates initial suspicion from permanent removal and requires
fresh evidence of the same component across two challenges and distinct audit
cycles for statistical confirmation.
\item \textbf{Bounded mitigation and rehabilitation.} We retain strong evidence
across ambiguous cycles while allowing unconfirmed quarantine to expire and
clearing statistical memory after repeated clean observations.
\item \textbf{Evaluation across four free-rider behaviors.} We study unchanged-model
responses, random fabrication, history averaging, and trajectory imitation on
MNIST and CIFAR-10 under IID and non-IID partitions, with 30\% and 40\% free riders.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Free-Rider Attacks and Evasion}
Lin \emph{et al.} examined fabricated updates based on random weights or
differences between received global models, without local training
data~\cite{lin2019freeriders}. Their experiments showed that random weights
can evade autoencoder detection, while model-difference updates can evade
DAGMM. A detector that catches one fabrication strategy may therefore fail
when the attacker changes how its update is constructed.

Fraboni \emph{et al.} provided a theoretical and experimental account of free
riding under iterative aggregation~\cite{fraboni2021freerider}. Stochastic
disguises allowed non-contributing clients to approach the model obtained
by honest participants. Zhu \emph{et al.} studied the evolution of local and
global models through an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process and developed attacks
that account for both convergence and concealment~\cite{zhu2021advanced}.
For detection, the difficulty is clear: a fabricated response can follow
the same broad trajectory as a trained one. Similarity to ordinary updates
is therefore weak evidence of genuine work, just as an unusual update
need not establish free riding.

\subsection{Learned Detection and Contribution Auditing}
STD-DAGMM, proposed by Lin \emph{et al.}, combines an autoencoder and mixture
model with the standard deviation of submitted
updates~\cite{lin2019freeriders,zong2018dagmm}. The added statistic improved separation
where DAGMM alone struggled. That improvement comes with an auxiliary model
to fit and retain, adding computation and memory beyond the update
statistics themselves. The cost depends on the architecture and fitting
schedule. Our measurements are reported separately in
Section~\ref{sec:native-timing}. Lin \emph{et al.} considered differing local
data distributions, although their experiments used MNIST and a shallow
fully connected model. An anomaly score also remains an indirect signal:
it identifies a response that differs from its peers, without observing
whether local training actually occurred.

FRAD extends learned detection with contribution and reputation.
Wang \emph{et al.} model contribution through computing resources,
communication cost and data quality, then use PageRank-based reputation
to guide participation~\cite{wang2024frad}. Historical information gives
the detector more context than a single update. It also creates a practical
requirement for reliable estimates of those quantities, while retaining
DAGMM fitting. A low contribution ranking, on its own, does not explain
whether a client withheld training or simply had less to contribute.

PASS takes a different route. Clients evaluate other participants' updates
on their own private datasets and report accuracy-based contribution
scores~\cite{wang2024pass}. The method addresses both anonymous and selfish
free riders, including evaluated cases with an attacker majority. Auditing
stays at the clients, so it does not require the server to inspect their raw
data. Participating devices must nevertheless evaluate additional models,
receive other clients' updates and report audit scores. Sharing individual
updates with peers also extends their exposure beyond the server. PASS
addresses this risk through Gaussian perturbation and parameter pruning,
making privacy protection and audit utility part of the same design choice.
An accuracy-based audit measures usefulness on the evaluator's data.
Under heterogeneous data, poor utility alone is not conclusive evidence
that the sender skipped training.

\subsection{Canary-Based Evidence and Privacy Tradeoffs}
Recasens \emph{et al.} introduced FRIDA, which uses membership and property
inference to look for evidence of client
training~\cite{recasens2026frida}. Its membership-based variants ask clients
to train on a small labeled canary set supplied by the server. The server
then tests returned models using canary losses or gradient similarity.
This creates a deliberate training signal, but clients must perform extra
canary training and the server must hold suitable examples and evaluate
responses. The variants also behave differently under evasion. In the
authors' canary-only training experiment, the cosine-based test was evaded,
whereas the loss-based test retained separation.

FRIDA's property-based variants avoid client-side canary training by
inferring characteristics such as label distributions from
updates~\cite{recasens2026frida}. They move work to the server and use
information about local datasets as detection evidence. Raw records need
not leave the client, yet inferred properties can still be sensitive.
The authors examine privacy protections, including local differential
privacy. The tradeoff concerns what can be inferred from an update, not
direct access to the underlying records.

There is a related constraint on all methods that inspect individual
responses. Secure aggregation, developed by Bonawitz \emph{et al.},
conceals those responses while revealing their
aggregate~\cite{bonawitz2017practical}. A detector that needs attributable
updates cannot operate unchanged behind that protection. BaitFL has this
limitation too. Keeping raw data local does not, by itself, make the
submitted model private.

\subsection{Robust Aggregation and Heterogeneous Training}
Robust aggregation addresses corrupted contributions rather than directly
verifying participation. Krum selects updates through pairwise
distances~\cite{blanchard2017krum}. Coordinatewise median and trimmed
mean resist contaminated coordinates~\cite{yin2018byzantine}, while
RFA uses a geometric-median oracle that can build on secure averaging
\cite{pillutla2022rfa}. These methods can limit harmful influence without
establishing whether a participant trained. A free rider whose response
lies inside the accepted update population may still benefit from the
shared model.

FLTrust instead trains a server model on a small trusted root dataset
and uses its update to assess client directions~\cite{cao2021fltrust}.
That trusted-data assumption differs from BaitFL's population-based
comparison. Neither design establishes that a response with a plausible
direction necessarily came from local optimization.

Legitimate heterogeneity also matters. FedProx limits local drift with a
proximal objective, SCAFFOLD uses control variates, and FedNova addresses
objective inconsistency from unequal local work
\cite{li2020fedprox,karimireddy2020scaffold,wang2020fednova}.
Such methods improve the training process. They do not, by themselves,
identify clients that skipped it. Combining auditing with a different
optimizer would require reevaluating both honest-response variation and
fabricated responses.

\subsection{Verifiable Training and Contribution Valuation}
Proof-of-learning approaches retain evidence from the optimization
trajectory. Jia \emph{et al.} use training records and partial replay to
verify claimed work~\cite{jia2021prooflearning}. VeriML uses cryptographic
proofs for selected committed training iterations in outsourced machine
learning~\cite{zhao2019veriml}. These approaches offer a different form
of assurance, with requirements for producing, retaining and checking
training evidence. BaitFL instead assesses returned models and does not
provide a cryptographic proof of computation.

Contribution valuation asks a related but distinct question: how much a
participant's data or update improves a chosen utility. Data Shapley and
efficient Shapley approximations formalize this allocation problem
\cite{ghorbani2019datashapley,jia2019datavaluation}. GTG-Shapley reduces
the expense through gradient-based model reconstruction, guided sampling
and truncation~\cite{liu2022gtgshapley}. Marginal utility is not evidence
of training effort. Honest clients can contribute little on a particular
evaluation distribution, and a fabricated response can still receive a
nonzero valuation.

These studies provide several forms of evidence, each with a cost:
auxiliary fitting, peer evaluation, proof verification, model evaluation
or inference about private data. The question pursued here is whether useful checks can be
built into ordinary model exchanges without adding an auxiliary detector
or a separate client-side audit task. BaitFL examines responses to hidden
model challenges and requires repeated evidence before removal. Its
challenge responses are withheld from aggregation, however, so the cost
of those checks and their effect on honest participation remain part of
the evaluation.

The comparisons serve different purposes. We measure BaitFL's detection
and utility under the stated attacks, compare utility with no-defense
aggregation, and profile a STD-DAGMM implementation for timing.
Table~\ref{tab:method-comparison} compares published computation and
information requirements. It is not an empirical ranking of detection
accuracy, runtime or privacy across all the listed methods.

\section{Threat Model}\label{sec:threat}
\subsection{Participants and Server Knowledge}
A trusted server coordinates $N$ clients with persistent identities and
reliable communication. Every active client responds in each round.
The population is fixed, with no client sampling, late arrivals or dropouts.
Removal ends participation. Quarantined clients still return responses,
but those responses are excluded from aggregation, as are all responses to
traps. Honest clients train the model they receive with the prescribed
optimizer. Their datasets may differ in size and class distribution,
covering both IID and non-IID partitions.

The server may send different models to different clients without disclosing
which are traps. For client $i$ in round $t$, it knows the sent model,
$w^{\rm send}_{t,i}$, and the returned model, $w^{\rm ret}_{t,i}$. These give
the update and its norm,
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_{t,i}=w^{\rm ret}_{t,i}-w^{\rm send}_{t,i},\qquad
 r_{t,i}=\|\Delta_{t,i}\|_2.
\end{equation}
Detection uses these responses and the server's audit history. The server
has no access to private training records and no prior knowledge of which
clients are free riders or how they construct their responses.
Individual responses are visible to it, so the setting does not use
secure aggregation.

\subsection{Free-Rider Objective and Scope}
Free riders seek the shared model while avoiding local training costs.
Some lack suitable data or training resources. Others have both but choose
to withhold them. They can still receive and store models and carry out
the relatively simple calculations needed to fabricate a response.
Introducing a targeted error or backdoor is outside their objective here.

Our experiments consider persistent free riding without collusion,
following Lin \emph{et al.}~\cite{lin2019freeriders}. These clients perform
no local optimization at any stage. Partial training and behavior that
alternates between training and free riding fall outside this scope.
Each attacker relies only on its own received-model history and local
randomness. They do not share responses or change identities.
The evaluated strategies do not attempt to recognize traps or adapt to
evade audits. Section~\ref{sec:evasion} discusses those possibilities.

We assume an honest majority because screening uses peer statistics and
seeks a separated minority of suspicious responses. The experiments use
30\% and 40\% free riders, with one fixed fabrication strategy shared by
the free riders in each run.

\subsection{Evaluated Free-Rider Behaviors}
Let $\widehat w_{t,i}$ denote the model returned by a free rider.
All four strategies below skip local optimization.

\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{FR1: current-model sendback.}\par\nopagebreak\smallskip\noindent
Returning the received model unchanged gives a zero update, whether the
model is ordinary or a trap:
\begin{equation}
 \widehat w_{t,i}=w^{\rm send}_{t,i}.
\end{equation}

\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{FR2: bounded random fabrication.}\par\nopagebreak\smallskip\noindent
Independent uniform noise is added to each floating point entry of the
received model state:
\begin{equation}
 \widehat w_{t,i}=w^{\rm send}_{t,i}+\epsilon_{t,i},\quad
 (\epsilon_{t,i})_j\sim\mathcal U(-10^{-3},10^{-3}).
\end{equation}

\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{FR3: received-history averaging.}\par\nopagebreak\smallskip\noindent
With a history length $H$, the response averages the most recent
$\min(H,t)$ received model states, stored in $\mathcal H_{t,i}$:
\begin{equation}
 \widehat w_{t,i}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal H_{t,i}|}
       \sum_{w\in\mathcal H_{t,i}}w.
\end{equation}
This history includes the current model and any earlier traps. The client
therefore follows recent model evolution without training on local data.

\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{FR4: received-trajectory imitation.}\par\nopagebreak\smallskip\noindent
Changes between consecutive received models provide an estimated update.
Let $\overline d_{t,i}$ average up to the five most recent differences:
\begin{align}
 d_{s,i}&=w^{\rm send}_{s,i}-w^{\rm send}_{s-1,i},\\
 \widehat w_{t,i}&=w^{\rm send}_{t,i}+\overline d_{t,i}+\eta_{t,i}.
\end{align}
For a standard Gaussian vector $z_{t,i}$, the noise is scaled as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fr4-noise}
 \eta_{t,i}=0.1\,
 \frac{\max(\|\overline d_{t,i}\|_2,\epsilon_0)}
      {\max(\|z_{t,i}\|_2,\epsilon_0)}\,z_{t,i},
 \qquad\epsilon_0=10^{-6}.
\end{equation}
The vectors cover all floating model entries. On the first response,
$\overline d_{1,i}=0$ and $\eta_{1,i}$ instead has independent Gaussian entries with
standard deviation $10^{-4}$. Traps enter the same history as ordinary
models and affect the estimated trajectory.

\section{Proposed Methodology}\label{sec:method}
BaitFL compares clients through hidden model challenges, then separates
initial suspicion from the evidence needed for removal. Figure~\ref{fig:baitfl-methodology}
shows the four phases. Warmup establishes an ordinary training trajectory
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:warmup}). Paired coverage collects comparable responses
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:paired}), and confirmation updates the client records
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:confirmation}). Outside paired cycles, ordinary training
continues with sparse audits (Algorithm~\ref{alg:sparse}).

The server first runs warmup and initial paired coverage. Thereafter, it
repeats paired cycles while any client has an active review budget or
rehabilitation probation. Otherwise, it executes ordinary rounds and any
scheduled sparse audit until the training horizon $T$ is reached. These
transitions are the feedback arrows in Figure~\ref{fig:baitfl-methodology}.
The records $\mathcal S$ retain client status, quarantine, review budget,
component counts, clean progress and cooldown. Counts start at zero and
the quiet clock is initially unset, $\sigma=\bot$. The study uses warmup length
$T_{\rm warm}=10$, paired-cycle length $\ell_{\rm cyc}=10$ and sparse-audit interval $I=10$.

The challenge changes a client's starting point without changing the
requested training task. Honest optimization responds to that starting
point through the local data. An unchanged response, arbitrary noise or
recycled model history need not respond in the same way. Comparing
clients that received the same challenge can expose differences in the
size and layer distribution of their updates. Two independent challenges
test whether the difference recurs. This is behavioral evidence rather
than proof of training, since fabricated and genuine responses may still
overlap.

\subsection{Challenges, Measurements, and Aggregation}
Each paired cycle starts from a frozen copy $w^{(c)}$ of the current global
model. For floating tensor $k$, the server creates traps $A$ and $B$ as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trap}
 \tau_{a,k}=w^{(c)}_k+
 \gamma\max\{\operatorname{sd}(w^{(c)}_k),\epsilon_\tau\}\xi_{a,k},
 \quad a\in\{A,B\}.
\end{equation}
Here $\xi_{a,k}\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ uses independent draws, and
$\operatorname{sd}$ is the tensor's population standard deviation. The
study sets $\gamma=0.01$ and $\epsilon_\tau=10^{-4}$. Floating buffers,
including BatchNorm statistics, are perturbed along with weights.
Integer counters remain unchanged. The two traps stay fixed within the
cycle, even as ordinary responses update the global model.

For a response to trap $a$, write $\Delta^a_{i,k}$ for the returned tensor
minus the tensor sent to client $i$. Its norm $r_i^a$ measures total update
magnitude. The normalized tensor profile is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:profile}
 p^a_{i,k}=\frac{\|\Delta^a_{i,k}\|_2}
                  {\sum_j\|\Delta^a_{i,j}\|_2}.
\end{equation}
The profile is zero when its denominator is below $10^{-6}$. Otherwise,
it records where the update magnitude lies across floating tensors.

For each trap, client $i$ is compared with all other active clients having
a selected response to that same trap. Quarantined and unresolved clients
are not removed from this reference. Let $m_i^a$ and $d_i^a$ be the median
and median absolute deviation (MAD) of the peer norms. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:norm-score}
 z_i^{n,a}=\frac{r_i^a-m_i^a}{d_i^a/\Phi^{-1}(0.75)},
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function.
The factor normalizes the MAD. It does not assume that client updates
are normally distributed.

The profile reference $\widetilde p_{-i}^a$ is the coordinatewise median
of the peer profiles. The implementation uses the lower middle value
when a tensor coordinate has an even number of peer observations.
Define the client's profile distance as
$\rho_i^a=\|p_i^a-\widetilde p_{-i}^a\|_2$. Peer distances are computed
against that same reference. Their median $\mu_i^a$ and MAD $\delta_i^a$
give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:profile-score}
 z_i^{p,a}=\frac{\rho_i^a-\mu_i^a}
                  {\delta_i^a/\Phi^{-1}(0.75)}.
\end{equation}
A missing comparison or zero MAD leaves the corresponding score undefined.

Send-back is checked separately. It means an exactly zero floating-point
update norm, or a returned model whose fingerprint matches the immediately
previous model sent to that client. The latter comparison covers every
tensor in the model state. The server retains one previous-sent fingerprint
per client, not a history of full returned models.

Aggregation runs alongside these checks. Let $\mathcal A_t$ contain clients
that received the ordinary model and were not quarantined at the start of
round $t$, excluding any newly quarantined or removed at its end. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method-aggregation}
 w_{t+1}=w_t+\frac{1}{|\mathcal A_t|}
                 \sum_{i\in\mathcal A_t}\Delta_{t,i}.
\end{equation}
The update applies to floating tensors with equal client weights.
This gives each eligible client equal influence, unlike sample-size
weighting in the usual FedAvg formulation~\cite{mcmahan2017communication}.
The server does not need client sample counts. Both the detector study
and its no-defense comparison use this same weighting, so their utility
differences are evaluated under a common aggregation objective.
Non-floating entries retain their server values. An empty $\mathcal A_t$
leaves the model unchanged. Training on a trap replaces ordinary local
training in that round. The trap response never enters aggregation.

\subsection{Phase I: Warmup}
The warmup box in Figure~\ref{fig:baitfl-methodology} corresponds to
Algorithm~\ref{alg:warmup}. All clients receive the ordinary global model
and return responses for aggregation
(lines~\ref{ln:warm:send}--\ref{ln:warm:aggregate}). The server records update
summaries and maintains the previous-sent fingerprints, but makes no
removal decision. Evidence counters start at zero.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
\caption{Phase I: ordinary warmup}\label{alg:warmup}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]\small
\Procedure{Warmup}{$w,\mathcal S$}
 \For{$t=1,\ldots,T_{\rm warm}$}
  \State Send $w$ to all active clients\label{ln:warm:send}
  \State Collect responses, summaries and send-back checks
  \State $\mathcal A_t\gets$ all active clients
  \State $w\gets\Call{Aggregate}{w,\mathcal A_t}$\label{ln:warm:aggregate}
 \EndFor
 \State \Return $(w,T_{\rm warm},\mathcal S)$
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Warmup ends without selecting trusted clients. The next phase starts
with the entire active population and zero confirmation counts.

\subsection{Phase II: Paired Coverage and Screening}
The paired-challenge, response-summary and comparison boxes in
Figure~\ref{fig:baitfl-methodology} form this phase. Initially, clients are
shuffled into at most ten groups. Pairs of groups receive one trap, then
the opposite trap in the second half of the cycle. Every client receives
both. With 100 clients, about 20\% receive a trap in each initial-coverage
round. The rest receive the current global model.

Later cycles reuse Algorithm~\ref{alg:paired}, with different scheduling
for clients under review. For each client and trap, one response position
is chosen uniformly from its scheduled positions before responses are
observed (line~\ref{ln:paired:select}). Only those preselected summaries
enter the cycle decision. Missing paired coverage cannot authorize removal.
After a complete cycle, scoring and confirmation precede its final
aggregation (lines~\ref{ln:paired:screen}--\ref{ln:paired:filter}).

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
\caption{Phase II: collect and assess paired responses}\label{alg:paired}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]\small
\Procedure{PairedCycle}{$w,t,\mathcal S,c,\sigma,\mathrm{initial}$}
 \State Create fixed independent traps $A,B$ from a copy of $w$
 \If{$\mathrm{initial}$}
  \State Shuffle groups and schedule one response to each trap
 \Else
  \State Group active-review and remaining clients separately
  \State Alternate traps for reviewed clients throughout the cycle
  \State Schedule each remaining client once on each trap
 \EndIf
 \State Preselect one position per client and trap\label{ln:paired:select}
 \State Initialize selected summaries $O$
 \State $h\gets\min(\ell_{\rm cyc},T-t)$
 \For{$s=1,\ldots,h$}
  \State Advance $t$ and read trap recipients $\mathcal T_t$
  \State Send the trap to $\mathcal T_t$ and $w$ to others
  \State Collect responses and retain preselected summaries
  \State $\mathcal A_t\gets$ ordinary, non-quarantined recipients at round start
  \If{$s=\ell_{\rm cyc}$}
   \State Require complete A/B coverage of active clients
   \State $\mathcal E\gets\Call{ScreenAndCertify}{O}$\label{ln:paired:screen}
   \State $\mathcal S\gets\Call{Confirm}{\mathcal S,\mathcal E,c}$\label{ln:paired:confirm}
   \State Exclude new removals and quarantines from $\mathcal A_t$\label{ln:paired:filter}
  \EndIf
  \State $w\gets\Call{Aggregate}{w,\mathcal A_t}$
 \EndFor
 \If{the cycle completed}
  \State Advance $c$ and clear the completed schedule
  \State $\sigma\gets t$ if no review/probation remains, else $\bot$
 \EndIf
 \State \Return $(w,t,\mathcal S,c,\sigma)$
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Screening}
For each client, form $D_i^A=z_i^{p,A}-z_i^{n,A}$,
$D_i^B=z_i^{p,B}-z_i^{n,B}$ and their average. Each view uses
$\lceil\sqrt{n_v}\rceil$ equally spaced histogram bins, where $n_v$ counts
clients with valid paired scores. Otsu's criterion selects a
split~\cite{otsu1979threshold}. The lower side of the average-view split
must contain a majority of all active clients, not merely a majority of
the valid-score subset.

The server resamples clients jointly across the three views in 1,000
bootstrap replicates, obtaining a 99\% interval for each split
boundary~\cite{efron1979bootstrap}. A client above every upper boundary
is nominated as suspicious. One below every lower boundary is clear.
Other outcomes remain unresolved. These intervals describe split
stability, not a calibrated false-removal probability.

If the population split is unavailable or unstable, negative norm-z scores
on both traps nominate suspicion. Here zero denotes the measured peer
median. Neither score negative gives a clear result. One negative score
or an undefined comparison leaves the result unresolved. Paired send-back evidence is handled
independently of this statistical fallback.

\subsubsection{Geometric evidence}
A statistical nomination must pass a separate separation test. Let
$\mathcal U$ be the nominated cohort and $\mathcal V$ the remaining active
clients. With $n$ active clients, statistical certification requires
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cohort-support}
 \left\lceil\frac{n}{\lceil\sqrt n\rceil}\right\rceil
       \leq|\mathcal U|<|\mathcal V|.
\end{equation}
The minimum support grows approximately with $\sqrt n$, while the strict
minority condition reflects the assumed honest majority. This is a
population-based support rule, not an estimate of the attacker share.
For a given signal, write $Q_q(\mathcal U)$ for its empirical quantile
and $I_U=Q_{.75}(\mathcal U)-Q_{.25}(\mathcal U)$ for its interquartile
range (IQR). Low raw update norms require both
\begin{align}\label{eq:norm-certificate}
 Q_{.25}(\mathcal V)-Q_{.75}(\mathcal U)&>I_U,\\
 \min(\mathcal V)-Q_{.75}(\mathcal U)&>I_U.\nonumber
\end{align}
The individual norm must also lie below the peer median.

For high raw profile distances, let
$I_V=Q_{.75}(\mathcal V)-Q_{.25}(\mathcal V)$ and
$g=Q_{.25}(\mathcal U)-Q_{.75}(\mathcal V)$. Require $g>I_U$ and $g>0$,
together with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:profile-certificate}
 Q_{.25}(\mathcal U)-\max(\mathcal V)>I_U
 \quad\text{or}\quad
 \frac{I_U}{|\mathcal U|-1}<\frac{I_V}{|\mathcal V|-1}.
\end{equation}
The individual's profile distance must exceed the peer median. Constant
populations, invalid measurements or insufficient cohort support do not
produce a statistical certificate.

The norm test requires the suspicious cohort to remain separated from
the nearest peer, making overlap sufficient to reject certification.
The profile test permits either full separation or a more concentrated
cohort after the interquartile gap is established. Dividing each IQR by
population size minus one compares typical spacing rather than spread
alone. This allowance limits the influence of extreme peer distances,
but can also admit persistent honest profile outliers. It is a geometric
heuristic, not a calibrated false-positive test.

The same component must certify a client on both traps. The diagonal
score nominates clients but cannot itself certify removal. Paired
send-back supplies a separate evidence component without the statistical
cohort requirement. Each client receives one of three cycle outcomes,
$\mathsf{strong}$, $\mathsf{unresolved}$ or $\mathsf{clear}$.
A nomination that fails certification remains unresolved without
automatic quarantine. The collection of paired decisions is $\mathcal E$.
These are cycle outcomes. A client's retained status may still reflect
its recovery history.

\subsection{Phase III: Confirmation and Bounded Review}
The confirmation and evidence-memory boxes in
Figure~\ref{fig:baitfl-methodology} represent Algorithm~\ref{alg:confirmation}.
Separate counts $M_{i,n}$ and $M_{i,p}$ retain norm and profile certificates.
Only a fresh paired certificate increments its own component, once per
distinct complete cycle (line~\ref{ln:confirm:increment}). The confirming
cycles need not be consecutive. An ambiguous cycle preserves these
counts, while two consecutive complete clear cycles reset them.
Norm and profile evidence never combine into one confirmation.

Removal requires a fresh certificate whose component count reaches two
(line~\ref{ln:confirm:remove}). Retained evidence alone cannot remove a
client. Exact send-back follows a different rule: the paired condition
must hold in two consecutive complete cycles. A missing or interrupted
pair breaks that streak. Under the study's warmup and cycle lengths, the
earliest removal is round 30.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
\caption{Phase III: confirm evidence and bound review}\label{alg:confirmation}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]\small
\Procedure{Confirm}{$\mathcal S,\mathcal E,c$}
 \State Require one complete decision per active client and a newer cycle
 \ForAll{active clients $i$}
  \State $J_i\gets$ statistical components certified on both traps
  \State Advance or reset the consecutive paired send-back streak
  \If{the cycle result is $\mathsf{clear}$}
   \State Advance the consecutive-clear certificate-clean streak
   \State Clear $M_{i,n},M_{i,p}$ after two consecutive clear cycles
  \Else
   \State Reset the certificate-clean streak
   \State Increment $M_{i,j}$ once for each fresh $j\in J_i$\label{ln:confirm:increment}
  \EndIf
  \If{a fresh component reaches two, or send-back is confirmed}
   \State Permanently remove $i$\label{ln:confirm:remove}
  \ElsIf{strong paired evidence is present}
   \State Quarantine $i$ and renew its review budget to two cycles\label{ln:confirm:review}
   \State Reset recovery, probation and cooldown
  \Else
   \State Decrease the review budget toward zero
   \State Apply consecutive-clear rehabilitation and probation
   \If{unconfirmed review has expired}
    \State Release $i$ and set a two-audit cooldown\label{ln:confirm:release}
   \EndIf
  \EndIf
 \EndFor
 \State \Return $\mathcal S$
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Strong evidence starts quarantine and a two-cycle review budget
(line~\ref{ln:confirm:review}). Further fresh strong evidence renews it.
Without that renewal, the budget decreases after each completed cycle.
Expiry releases unconfirmed clients even if their evidence memory is
nonempty (line~\ref{ln:confirm:release}). An unresolved client may
therefore aggregate on ordinary rounds when it is not quarantined.

Two consecutive clear results rehabilitate a client under review or with
unresolved evidence. One additional clear cycle completes probation before sparse
auditing resumes. Unconfirmed release instead starts a cooldown lasting
two sparse audits. Fresh paired evidence can still start review during
that cooldown. Weak flags and stored counts cannot prolong exclusion
or keep dense probing active by themselves.

Clients with an active review budget and the remaining clients form
separate pools. At most ten groups are allocated proportionally, using
nearest rounding with ties down and bounds for the pool sizes. Reviewed
clients receive alternating traps throughout the cycle. Each remaining
client receives each trap once. This is the feedback to paired coverage
in Figure~\ref{fig:baitfl-methodology}: it reuses Algorithm~\ref{alg:paired}
with fresh traps and newly preselected positions.

\subsection{Phase IV: Ordinary Training and Sparse Audits}
When review and probation are empty, the server records the start of
quiet operation, $\sigma$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:sparse} then handles each
round. Ordinary responses continue to aggregate until a sparse audit is
due. The sparse-audit box in Figure~\ref{fig:baitfl-methodology} shows
this branch and its return to follow-up.

An audit sends one fresh trap to every active client. All responses are
excluded, leaving the global model unchanged for that round. Single-view
screening nominates clients, but only a strict norm/profile certificate
or exact send-back can open review. An eligible finding starts a
two-cycle review without immediate quarantine. Cooldown filtering occurs
at line~\ref{ln:sparse:eligible}, and review opens at
line~\ref{ln:sparse:enroll} of Algorithm~\ref{alg:sparse}. This brings the
next round back to paired coverage. The audit cannot increment or clear
statistical certificate memory, or authorize removal.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
\caption{Phase IV: ordinary rounds and sparse audits}\label{alg:sparse}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]\small
\Procedure{SparseRound}{$w,t,\mathcal S,\sigma$}
 \State $t\gets t+1$
 \If{active clients remain, $\sigma\ne\bot$ and $t-\sigma\geq I$}
  \State Send one fresh trap to all active clients
  \State Collect same-trap norm/profile scores and send-back checks\label{ln:sparse:measure}
  \State $\mathcal F\gets$ clients with a single-trap certificate or send-back
  \State $\mathcal D\gets$ clients currently in cooldown
  \State $\mathcal F_{\rm elig}\gets\mathcal F\setminus\mathcal D$\label{ln:sparse:eligible}
  \State Decrease all audit cooldowns toward zero
  \ForAll{$i\in\mathcal F_{\rm elig}$}
   \State Open two cycles of review without quarantine\label{ln:sparse:enroll}
   \State Reset clean progress and the paired send-back streak
  \EndFor
  \State $\sigma\gets t$ if $\mathcal F=\emptyset$, otherwise $\bot$\label{ln:sparse:clock}
  \State Keep $w$ unchanged
 \Else
  \State Send $w$ to active clients and collect responses
  \State $\mathcal A_t\gets$ eligible ordinary clients
  \State $w\gets\Call{Aggregate}{w,\mathcal A_t}$
 \EndIf
 \State \Return $(w,t,\mathcal S,\sigma)$
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The cooldown check uses its value before decrementing, so a released
client waits through two sparse audits before re-enrollment. Statistical
memory survives this enrollment, while the consecutive send-back streak
is reset. Line~\ref{ln:sparse:clock} follows the implemented rule and
restarts the quiet clock only when the raw finding set $\mathcal F$ is
empty. Cooldown filtering affects review enrollment rather than this
clock assignment. Appendix~\ref{app:audit-clock} examines the case in which
all findings are blocked by cooldown and checks the reported audits.

\subsection{Choice of Protocol Settings}
Table~\ref{tab:notation-settings} lists the settings shared by every
reported configuration. Ten groups and a ten-round cycle provide a simple
coverage schedule. Two challenges and two confirming cycles are the
smallest repeated-evidence design used here. Review and cooldown lengths
bound exclusion and immediate re-enrollment, while the additional clear
cycle extends monitoring before sparse auditing resumes. These are design tradeoffs,
not statistically optimal choices.

Perturbations are scaled to each tensor so that a common relative
strength can be used across layers. The scale floor handles tensors with
negligible variance. Bootstrap replicates assess split stability, and
the nominal confidence level controls the width of those split intervals.
It does not supply a false-removal probability. The defaults were adopted
during development and then held fixed across the reported grid. There
is no separately held-out tuning study or sensitivity experiment, so
the results do not establish an optimal parameter setting.

\section{Results and Discussion}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
The study covers MNIST and CIFAR-10, IID and Dirichlet non-IID partitions,
FR1--FR4, and free-rider proportions of 30\% and 40\%
\cite{lecun1998gradient,krizhevsky2009learning}. Each configuration is
evaluated across three random seeds, giving 96 detection runs in total.
The honest-client impact and resource tables average the completed
replications, while detection rates pool the removal counts. The
optimizer and detector settings are held fixed.

Every federation has 100 clients and runs for 100 communication rounds.
IID data are shuffled into equal-size client partitions. Non-IID label
proportions follow a Dirichlet distribution with $\alpha=0.5$, with
allocation repeated if any client receives no samples. Partitioning,
attacker identities, initialization, local training and audit scheduling
vary through separate random streams.

Honest clients perform three local SGD epochs, using learning rate 0.01,
momentum 0.9 and batch size 32. The MNIST network has convolutional blocks
with 16 and 32 channels and a 128-unit hidden classifier. CIFAR-10 uses
32, 64 and 128 channels with BatchNorm and a 256-unit hidden classifier.
Images are normalized without augmentation. Execution uses the CPU, with
six PyTorch threads. The evaluated FR3 history length is $H=5$.

Two comparisons serve different purposes. Completed, seed-matched
no-defense runs assess the effect of the whole BaitFL workflow.
Separately instrumented BaitFL and STD-DAGMM executions measure timing
stages over the full horizon. The latter are timing cases, separate from
the multi-seed detection averages.
STD-DAGMM is used for timing only. PASS, FRAD and FRIDA enter the
requirements comparison, not a matched detection benchmark.

\begingroup\color{black}
\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
The evaluation asks whether BaitFL identifies free riders, preserves
useful participation and adds little computational work. We assess these
questions through detection performance, model accuracy, honest-client
impact and resource use.

\textbf{Detection performance.} Precision measures the proportion of
permanently removed clients that are free riders, while recall measures
the proportion of free riders removed. We also report $F_1$ and the round at which removal
of all free riders is complete. An unresolved label or temporary quarantine
does not count as detection. If any free rider remains at the training
horizon, complete detection is recorded as not reached.

\textbf{Model accuracy and honest participation.} Global test accuracy
measures the utility of the trained model. Comparisons with no-defense
federated averaging report accuracy differences in percentage points.
To assess the cost to honest participants, we record permanent removals,
clients ever quarantined and clients still unresolved at completion.
Client-round counts measure how long responses are withheld. Total
withheld participation includes trap responses and quarantine, counting
each affected client-round once.

\textbf{Lightweightness.} Mean round time, execution duration and peak
process resident memory (RSS) describe the cost of the complete simulation.
The round timer covers training, response processing, detection,
aggregation and global evaluation, ending before log and checkpoint
publication. Execution duration also includes setup and publication work,
but excludes powered-off waiting. Separate instrumented runs divide the
work into computation stages and distinguish these from elapsed round
time, which also includes logging and other execution overhead. Timing means
cover all 100 rounds, spreading periodic decision costs over the full
training horizon. Percentage comparisons use the corresponding
no-defense or STD-DAGMM execution as the reference. Network transmission
is not measured in this local simulator.

Each configuration has three complete runs. We treat the run as the
experimental unit and report accuracy variation across those runs.
Pooled precision summarizes the observed client outcomes, not uncertainty
from independent client trials. Clients in a federation share a training
trajectory, so the pooled percentage is not a confidence bound.
\endgroup

\subsection{Honest-Client Impact}
The principal errors occur among honest clients with non-IID data.
Table~\ref{tab:safety} reports \MultiFP{} permanent honest removals and
\MultiQuarantined{} honest client-cases experiencing quarantine, totaling
620 quarantine client-rounds. No client remains quarantined at completion.
Table~\ref{tab:safety}(a) covers FR1--FR4 in every setting.
Panel (b) lists every configuration with an honest removal, quarantine
or ordinary-response exclusion. All other configurations have zero
counts for these events. Entries are per-run means, and brackets give
the observed minimum and maximum counts across seeds. A client-round
is one honest client in one round. The withheld-response percentage
counts traps and quarantine without double counting, relative to active
honest client-rounds. Ordinary exclusions count only non-trap responses.

The decision records identify the immediate causes of the false positives.
Two honest clients in MNIST non-IID FR3 receive profile-distance
certificates in successive cycles and are removed at round 30. A third
honest client, in FR4, receives norm certificates in separated cycles
and is removed at round 90. None of these removals follows exact sendback.
Non-IID training can produce legitimate updates whose size or distribution
across layers differs persistently from the population. When these updates
also satisfy the geometric gate, repeated confirmation can reinforce the
same mistaken suspicion. Fresh traps do not remove the underlying
similarity, because a client's local data remain unchanged. This is a
limitation of inferring training behavior from response statistics.

Unresolved labels can persist after the free riders have gone.
For non-IID FR3 at 40\%, the mean numbers of honest clients still
unresolved at completion are 37 on MNIST and 43 on CIFAR-10, respectively
61.7\% and 71.7\% of the 60 honest clients. Unresolved denotes remaining
response evidence rather than a free-rider classification.
Ordinary responses from these clients remain
eligible for aggregation, and they remain in the same-trap reference
population. The label alone neither renews quarantine nor initiates dense
probing, which depend on an active review budget or recovery probation.
Such widespread unresolved labels have little value as an end-of-run
classifier of free riding in these settings.
Most withheld honest responses instead arise from the planned trap
schedule. Even IID settings with no honest removal or quarantine withhold
roughly 11\% of honest responses. These forgone contributions are a
utility cost even when the client is never removed.

\section{Complexity and Lightweight Design}\label{sec:complexity}
\subsection{Computational Cost}
BaitFL reads model responses to extract small summaries, then compares
those summaries when challenge coverage is complete. Let $N$ be the
largest active population, $P$ the number of floating model coordinates,
and $L$ the number of floating tensors, including floating buffers.
The detector uses $B$ bootstrap replicates and
$K=\lceil\sqrt N\rceil$ histogram bins.
Table~\ref{tab:complexity} separates response processing from the periodic
decision work. Ordinary aggregation, local training and persistent
research logs are outside these incremental bounds.

\begin{table*}[t]\centering\footnotesize
\caption{Incremental detector computation and working state.}\label{tab:complexity}
\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.6cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.6cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.0cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{5.3cm}}\toprule
Operation & Work & Working state & Implementation \\ \midrule
Norms, profiles and fingerprints & $O(NP)$ per round & $O(P)$ scratch and $O(N)$ hashes & Read returned tensors without training an auxiliary model. \\
Selected challenge summaries & $O(NL)$ per paired cycle & $O(NL)$ & Retain two small profiles per client. \\
Leave-one-out comparisons & $O(N^2(L+\log N))$ per cycle & $O(NL)$ temporary & Form peer medians and distances for each client. \\
Histogram and bootstrap splits & $O(B(N+K))$ per cycle & $O(BN+BK)$ temporary & Resample scalar scores across three views. \\
Geometric tests and confirmation & $O(N)$ per cycle & $O(N)$ counters & Apply fixed-component tests and update evidence memory. \\
Shared challenge construction & $O(P)$ per cycle & $O(P)$ & Generate two shared models. \\
\bottomrule\end{tabular}\end{table*}

Response summaries require a pass over model coordinates. The more
important population-scaling cost lies in the leave-one-out comparisons:
each client is compared with the other $N-1$ profiles. This requires
$O(N^2L)$ coordinate work, with $O(N^2\log N)$ additional work from
repeated scalar medians in the current Python implementation. Bootstrap
resampling acts on scalar scores rather than full model vectors.

For $T$ rounds, $M$ complete paired cycles and $A$ sparse audits, a bound
on total detector work is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \mathcal W_{\rm det}=O\bigl(&TNP+(M+A)\bigl[N^2(L+\log N)\\
          &\hspace{4em}+B(N+K)\bigr]\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The audit term includes single-challenge scoring, which cannot itself
authorize removal. Dense monitoring increases response processing, but
only the preselected pair enters a complete-cycle decision.
Thus, the implementation is linear in model size for feature extraction
and quadratic in client count for peer comparison.

At 100 clients, the two-challenge comparison covers 158,400 profile
coordinates on MNIST and 435,600 on CIFAR-10, before constants for
medians and distances. Each bootstrap view draws 100,000 scalar indices
with the configured 1,000 replicates. These operations explain the small
feature representation. Their practical cost is measured separately in
Section~\ref{sec:native-timing}.

\subsection{Memory and Communication}
The detector's working state is
$O(P+NL+BN+BK)$, including shared challenges, paired summaries and
bootstrap temporaries. Evidence counters and fingerprints add $O(N)$.
Table~\ref{tab:payload} gives calculated payloads for the evaluated
architectures. Two float32 challenges occupy about 1.58 MiB on MNIST and
4.74 MiB on CIFAR-10. A packed representation of the paired summaries
requires about 8.59 and 19.53 KiB, respectively, at 100 clients.
The calculation uses $2N(L+3)\cdot4$ bytes, allowing three scalar slots
alongside each profile.
\input{generated/model_payload.tex}

These payloads describe packed numerical data, whereas the implementation
also allocates Python objects, dictionaries and temporary arrays.
Bootstrap gathers, indices and counts can coexist in memory. The full
simulator additionally retains model responses, attacker histories and
research logs. In dense probing, profile logs alone can grow as
$O(TNL)$. Consequently, the process RSS in Table~\ref{tab:cost} measures
the complete experiment rather than the detector's compact evidence
state.

Challenges replace ordinary models in the existing request/response
exchange. Clients follow the same local training procedure, and the
nominal model-payload order remains $O(NP)$ per round. Challenge
responses are withheld from aggregation, however, so an unchanged number
of exchanges does not establish an unchanged time to target accuracy.
Figure~\ref{fig:time-target} reports the observed cost at two illustrative
targets. Transport costs are not measured here.

The claim of lightweight server computation rests on compact response features, a fixed
number of evidence counters per client and the absence of auxiliary
learning. The measured checking costs support that description at the
evaluated scale. Larger populations still incur the quadratic peer
comparison cost, and network or energy savings require separate
measurements.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{Information Available to the Server}
BaitFL uses returned models, client and challenge identifiers, response
norms, profile distances and exact-sendback checks. Attacker identities
and behavior settings belong to the simulator and evaluation records.
They do not enter screening, geometric tests or confirmation. Likewise,
the configured attacker proportion does not set a removal quota.
This separation prevents direct use of evaluation labels, although the
method's design has been informed by experimental observations.

\subsection{Confirmation and Honest-Client Safety}
Statistical removal requires fresh evidence from the same component
on both selected challenges and in another complete cycle. Mixed
norm/profile findings cannot be combined to reach confirmation, and
stored evidence alone cannot authorize removal. Paired exact sendback
also requires repetition. Weak unresolved labels do not renew quarantine,
and unconfirmed review expires when fresh strong evidence does not recur.

These rules reduce reliance on isolated anomalies. They do not make
repeated evidence statistically independent. An honest client keeps
its local data across challenges and can therefore remain unusual in
successive cycles. Certificate memory can accumulate such findings
unless two complete clear cycles reset it, explaining the risk of late
false removal observed in the experiments.

The decision boundaries are derived from the observed population rather
than an attack-fitted numerical z-score cutoff. Nevertheless, the method
has design parameters, including challenge strength, bootstrap confidence,
cohort support and confirmation lengths. At 100 clients, statistical
certification requires a cohort of at least ten. A small group of free
riders that avoids exact sendback may therefore remain uncertified.

\subsection{Adaptive Attacks and Identifiability}\label{sec:evasion}
A response-only detector cannot distinguish two clients whose observable
histories are identical, even if one trained and the other fabricated
its responses. BaitFL therefore tests whether challenges expose
differences in behavior. Its certificates do not prove that local
computation occurred.

The evaluated free riders are not told which models are challenges.
An adaptive participant might still recognize repeated frozen models,
unusual perturbations or differences revealed through communication with
other clients. Reproducible simulation randomness also does not establish
unpredictability against an attacker who knows its configuration.
Deployment would require server-secret randomness and tests against
challenge-aware strategies. Secrecy alone would not guarantee that
challenge models are indistinguishable from ordinary ones.

\subsection{Privacy and Participation Limits}
The server inspects individual returned models, so the current protocol
cannot be used directly with standard secure
aggregation~\cite{bonawitz2017practical}. It does not access raw client
data or require reported training loss, but these restrictions do not
provide differential privacy or prevent information leakage through
updates~\cite{kairouz2021advances,zhu2019leakage,melis2019leakage}.

The reference population assumes an honest majority. Colluding clients
could share trained responses, while Sybil identities could distort the
reference distribution. Neither case is evaluated. The experiments also
assume complete participation. Incomplete challenge coverage cannot
certify removal, and the simulator stops on a nonfinite response rather
than providing a full malicious-message or dropout-handling protocol.
Checkpoint recovery restores the recorded training and evidence state
after an interruption. Authentication and availability defenses require
additional mechanisms.

\subsection{Evaluation Scope and Future Work}
The study evaluates three seeds per configuration, 100 clients, one
architecture per dataset and one non-IID concentration. The pooled
detection rates describe these experiments. They are not statistical
guarantees for new populations. The evaluation does not include an
independent held-out design study, and matched no-defense coverage is
partial. Timing uses one declared non-IID FR3 configuration on each
dataset. We make no comparative detection-accuracy claim against
STD-DAGMM, PASS, FRAD or FRIDA, and the privacy comparison concerns
information requirements rather than measured leakage.

Reducing honest-client errors is the first priority. Matched ablations
should separate the effects of norm evidence, layer profiles, challenge
strength and retained certificates. Honest-only populations and longer
runs would help assess whether persistent data heterogeneity produces
repeated false evidence. Broader tests should vary local training,
non-IID concentration, attacker history and noise, and model architecture.
For CIFAR-10, trainable-weight evidence should also be distinguished from
the contribution of BatchNorm buffers. Honest-only cases and 5--20\%
free-rider populations would test the cohort gate's operating range.
Published decaying-noise and scaled-delta strategies need separate tests
\cite{fraboni2021freerider,zhu2021advanced}, rather than being treated
as equivalent to FR1--FR4.

Adaptive free riders and changing client populations pose further
questions. Experiments with collusion, intermittent participation and
selective training should examine whether challenges remain informative
when clients can infer the audit schedule. Privacy-preserving auditing
also deserves investigation because the current method relies on
individual response visibility.
Benchmarks such as LEAF and FedScale offer natural next settings for
client heterogeneity and system variation
\cite{caldas2018leaf,lai2022fedscale}.

Larger-scale profiling should measure detector memory,
communication bytes and energy. Controlled time-to-accuracy comparisons
should extend Figure~\ref{fig:time-target} with an honest-only oracle
baseline without auditing to separate audit costs from removal benefits.
Reusing peer computations and streaming research logs may reduce server
cost, but those optimizations need their own implementation and evaluation.
The present timing results establish costs for the recorded workflows
and fitting budget, rather than a universal runtime advantage.

\subsection{Code and Evidence Availability}
The project repository is available at\par
\noindent{\footnotesize\urlstyle{same}\url{https://github.com/Aashnan-Rahman/Mitigating-Free-Rider}}\par
The study retains configurations, per-client decisions, per-round timing
and source hashes for reproducibility. A public immutable archive of
the exact manuscript evidence is not yet available.

\section{Conclusion}
BaitFL audits federated-learning responses through model challenges,
compact statistical features and repeated fresh evidence. Multi-seed
experiments on MNIST and CIFAR-10 achieve \MultiRecall\% recall across
\ReviewRunCount{} runs, with \MultiFP{} honest removals in
\ReviewAffectedRuns{} runs. These errors show the remaining difficulty of separating
fabrication from persistent non-IID variation.

Available matched no-defense comparisons show comparable or slightly higher
final accuracy, with incomplete replication coverage.
The separate instrumented runs place checking at 0.58\% and 0.89\% of
computation time, respectively, without auxiliary detector training.
Together, the results support response auditing that is lightweight in
server computation at the
evaluated scale. Improving honest-client protection while retaining
detection under adaptive behavior is the next step.

\section{Audit-Clock Check}\label{app:audit-clock}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:sparse} uses the implemented clock rule. The clock
restarts when $\mathcal F$ is empty and is otherwise unset. Review uses
$\mathcal F_{\rm elig}$. If findings exist but all are blocked by
cooldown, no review opens and the unset clock can prevent later audits.

None of the \ReviewAuditCount{} recorded audits across \ReviewRunCount{}
runs has this pattern. There are \ReviewEmptyAudits{} empty audits.
In each of the other \ReviewNonemptyAudits{}, the finding count exceeds
the number of active clients in cooldown, ensuring an eligible finding.
Restarting the clock when $\mathcal F_{\rm elig}$ is empty would handle
the blocked-findings case. This alternative agrees with the implemented
guard on all recorded audits but has not been used to recompute the
training results. Agreement on these traces does not establish
equivalence for other cooldown states.
